## Abstract (user's draft, minimal edits; changed words marked **bold**, numbers filled from facts.md)

Interactive video world models generate videos conditioned on an initial image, a text prompt, and user actions. To be playable they must respond at frame rate, often on the user's own device, which calls for a small few-step causal model. Such models stream chunk by chunk but accumulate errors because each generated chunk conditions all subsequent ones: objects that leave the view are forgotten, and processes that continue out of sight stop. **Large** many-step **models** preserve this world state but are too slow for interactive generation. We introduce \textbf{SpecWM}, in which the large model does not generate the stream but keeps a lightweight causal **stream** consistent. The lightweight **pass** drafts every **chunk**; the large model checks several drafts at once and, where it disagrees, feeds its correction back into the draft's state, so later frames build on what the large model endorses rather than on the draft's drift. Unlike prior speculative video generation, which drafts to speed up a large model and admits drafts by an external quality score, SpecWM judges drafts by the large model's own denoising error and corrects the state the small model conditions on. \chk{This stays cheap: drift is concentrated in a few **chunks** — most chunks are accepted after one or two of four steps (mean 2.9) — and a corrected draft stays on track for many seconds.} On StEvo-Bench, SpecWM **matches the large model's physical-consistency rate (\todo{0.71 vs 0.68 physical inaccuracy; the judge's task-success rate is 0 for every model, so "success rate" needs a different metric})** while running **1.67**$\times$ faster than the target **at its quality, and 3.85$\times$ faster with a cloud/device resolution split at the quality of the fastest on-device game world model**.

`[NOTE on the two words I could not keep]` (1) "bidirectional": the large model that actually runs the loop is the 14B *causal* few-step model; the bidirectional 70-step Base takes 2,700 s per clip and is not in any measured result. (2) "lightweight model drafts every frame": in the measured system the draft is the large model's own first one or two denoising steps (the 1.3B drafter is the negative result, facts §16: its chunks are 0.63 σ from the target trajectory and a physics verifier makes the loop slower than the target). ## 1. Introduction

Interactive world models turn a single image, a prompt and a stream of user actions into a video that the user steers as it is generated [LingBot-World, Matrix-Game 2.0, Waypoint-1.5, Genie 3, Oasis]. Two requirements pull in opposite directions. The model must be *causal and few-step* so that the next chunk of frames is ready before the user notices — at 16 fps a chunk of 12 frames must be produced in 0.75 s — and it must be *consistent*: a bottle that rolls off the table should still be on the floor when the camera comes back, and water that was pouring should have filled the jar. Consistency comes from capacity and from denoising steps; both cost time. The field has answered with distillation: a bidirectional many-step teacher is distilled into a causal few-step student [Self Forcing, CausVid, Causal Forcing], and the student is then shrunk further for the device [Waypoint-1.5-1B, Matrix-Game 2.0]. The result is fast but drifts: on 43 camera-controlled prompts a 1.3B four-step causal model scores 5.83 against 7.51 for the 14B model of the same family, and its drift score is 75 against 51 [§12].

**Speculation for video.** Speculative decoding in language models lets a small drafter propose tokens that a large target model verifies in parallel, so the output follows the target's distribution at the drafter's speed [Leviathan et al., Chen et al.]. The analogy for video is tempting: let a small causal model draft chunks and let the large model check them. Two things break the analogy. First, a chunk is a continuous latent tensor, not a token with a likelihood, so there is no exact acceptance test; prior work substitutes an external image-quality score [SDVG]. Second, and more fundamentally, verification only pays if drafts are usually acceptable. We measure how far a 1.3B drafter's chunk lies from the 14B's own trajectory in the 14B's denoising space and find it is not close: 0.63 σ away, with 7 % of tokens inside the tolerance that the 14B's own first denoising step already satisfies for 36 % of tokens [§4]. Every verifier we tried — the 14B's one-step disagreement, a latent critic, ImageReward as in SDVG, and a physics-consistency VLM — either accepts drifted drafts or rejects almost all of them, and in the latter case the loop is slower than the large model alone (138 s per clip against 60 s) [§16]. The drafter is the problem, not the verifier.

**Our approach.** We therefore let the large model draft for itself. A few-step causal model denoises each chunk through K steps; we observe that for most chunks the latent has already converged after one or two steps, and that whether it has is *predictable from the model's hidden state*. We train a small convolutional head on the activations of a middle block to predict, for every token, the distance between the current estimate and the fully denoised chunk. At inference the head reads the draft after each step; when the predicted fraction of converged tokens exceeds a threshold the chunk is accepted, otherwise the model takes the next step. The head reaches AUROC 0.93–0.98 on held-out scenes and its predicted stopping depth, 2.22 steps, matches an oracle that sees the true gap (2.21) [§6, §17]. Verification costs under 1 % of a step and needs neither decoded frames nor a reward model.

Accepting a draft is only half of the problem; the other half is what the next chunk conditions on. A causal model carries its history in a key/value cache that is written when a chunk is committed. If the cache is written from the draft's noisy pass, the next chunk inherits the draft's error and the boundary between chunks shows as a jump (7–18 % of boundaries) [§10]. If it is always rewritten with a clean pass, the saving is lost. SpecWM uses a *safe commit*: a chunk accepted at step one or two is committed with one clean pass, a chunk that needed three or four steps keeps the cache of its last pass and refreshes only the newest latent frame. The result is jump-free (≤ 4 % of boundaries, as the full model) [§11] and the committed state is one the large model endorses.

**Where the time goes.** Speculation changes which model does the work, not where it runs. We measure what a 14B world model needs to run on a device: its key/value window, not its weights, dominates memory (14.2 GiB for a 18-frame window at 704×384 against 17.6 GiB of FP8 weights), and an FP8 window with 12 frames costs no quality [§18–19]. With this, the 14B runs on a 32 GB card at 17.5 fps with no cloud at all; on a 24 GB card it needs 4-bit weights and loses 0.14 in quality. For devices below that, we split the model by resolution rather than by step: the large model generates at 704×384 in the cloud, and the device only decodes and upsamples, which needs 0.5 GB and streams 0.27 MB per chunk. This mode runs at 15.7 fps (3.85× the full model) at the quality of the fastest on-device game world model (6.90 vs 6.91) with 20 % lower drift [§12].

**Contributions.**
1. A measurement that explains why small-drafter speculation fails for video world models: the drafter's chunks are outside the verifier's reach, independently of the verifier (§4, §16).
2. Self-speculation with a learned convergence head on the large model's activations and a safe commit rule for its state; teacher quality at 1.67× steady throughput, no decoded frames in the loop (§12, §17).
3. A device-fit study of the 14B model (KV window is the lever; fits 32 GB) and a resolution split for smaller devices at 3.85× (§18–19).
4. Latency reported in the standard world-model metrics (time to first frame, steady fps, action-to-frame p50) for every arm, including two game world models under the same camera paths (§13).

`[NOTE]` Diana asked for a formal argument for "stay within ε of the target trajectory". A candidate: for a flow-matching model, the error of stopping at step k is bounded by the remaining velocity integral, which the head estimates per token; the acceptance rule then bounds the per-chunk deviation by ε and the commit rule bounds how much of that deviation enters the cache. This is a sketch, not written.

## 2. Related work

**Interactive video world models.** Action- and camera-conditioned generators such as LingBot-World, Matrix-Game 2.0, Waypoint-1.5, Oasis and Genie 3 turn image-to-video diffusion models into controllable simulators. The fast ones are causal few-step models obtained by distillation from a bidirectional teacher — CausVid, Self Forcing and Causal Forcing distil with diffusion-matching objectives and train on the model's own rollouts to close the train/test gap; LongLive, Rolling Forcing and Self Forcing++ extend the horizon. All of them trade capacity for speed and inherit the drift of autoregressive generation; SpecWM keeps the large model in the loop instead of distilling it away.

**Speculative decoding beyond text.** Speculative decoding verifies a cheap drafter with an expensive target [Leviathan; Chen; Medusa; EAGLE; DFlash]. For diffusion and video there is no token likelihood to verify against: SDVG drafts chunks with a 1.3B model and admits them by ImageReward on decoded frames; Speculative Forcing exits tokens early inside one generator with a learned gap head and reports 1.35× on a 1.3B model, and finds chunk-wise acceptance worse. We also learn a gap head, but on the large model, at chunk granularity, and we add the commit rule that makes chunk-wise acceptance jump-free; our measurements of drafter distance explain why cross-model drafting underperforms.

**Fewer steps per chunk.** Feature caching (TeaCache, MagCache, TaylorSeer, EasyCache; WorldCache for world models) reuses or forecasts activations across the many steps of a 50-step sampler and gives 2–4× there; on a 4-step causal model there is at most one step to skip, and our measured gain on the few-step model was 1.2×. Early-exit and adaptive-step methods decide per sample how many steps to run [AdaDiff, Speculative Forcing]; our head decides per chunk from hidden activations and is trained on the model's own rollouts. Sparse and trainable attention (Sparse VideoGen, Radial, VSA, Sparse Forcing, Light Forcing) help at long contexts; at 3k tokens per chunk attention is not our bottleneck.

**Running large models on small devices.** Weight quantization (GPTQ, AWQ, SVDQuant/Nunchaku W4A4, FP8) and KV compression (Forcing-KV, VideoMLA, Future Forcing) reduce memory; we measure, for a 14B world model, that the KV window dominates and that an FP8 window with 12 latent frames is free. Cloud–edge collaboration for diffusion has split by denoising step (HybridSD, EC-Diff) or by keyframe (EcoVideo); our split is by resolution, which keeps the large model's trajectory intact and moves only decoding and upsampling to the device.

**Evaluation.** We follow StEvo-Bench's camera look-away protocol for consistency and physics, VBench-style quality judges, and the latency conventions of sdv2_latency (time to first frame, steady fps, action-to-frame latency).
