Abstract

Interactive video world models must render the consequence of each action at frame rate and keep the world consistent while doing so. Current models do one or the other. Few-step causal generators stream in real time, but each chunk they write into their cache conditions every later chunk, so an error is inherited rather than corrected: objects that leave the view are forgotten, and processes that continue out of sight stop. Large bidirectional models keep the world consistent, but they denoise an entire clip jointly and cannot show a frame before the clip is finished. We introduce Speculative World Models, a scheme that brings the draft and verify structure of speculative decoding to chunk-wise video generation. A small causal draft model proposes every chunk. A large target model checks each proposal with one forward pass at an intermediate noise level, at reduced resolution and with a short context, and regenerates only the chunks it rejects, conditioned on the accepted prefix. The corrected chunk is written into the draft's KV cache, so the draft continues from a state the target endorses rather than from its own drift. We derive the chunk-level acceptance rule from the maximal coupling of the two models' transition kernels at the verified noise level, and show why the same rule cannot be applied to whole clips: acceptance depends on the draft to target distance accumulated over every verified dimension and collapses as that number grows. On state evolution tasks from StEvo-Bench where the draft alone fails, Speculative World Models raise the success rate from a% to b% (target: c%) while calling the target on k% of chunks, running N× faster than the target at M fps on a single consumer GPU.

Method

Can the large model take part in streaming without being run on every chunk? Speculative decoding answers the analogous question for language models. Carrying it to a streaming world model runs into three obstacles, and the three design decisions below answer them.

Obstacle 1: where do the two models share a transition?

A token has a probability under both models; a chunk of video does not, unless the two models share a kernel. A bidirectional target that denoises a clip jointly over fifty steps and a causal draft that denoises one chunk in four share none. Answer: verify at an intermediate noise level. The few-step student re-noises its clean estimate with fresh Gaussian noise between nodes, and a target asked for a clean estimate at the same state defines a Gaussian with the same variance. At that node the two differ only in the mean, and the exact rule min{1, p/q} applies as written, with expected acceptance 2Φ(−d/2σ).

Obstacle 2: exact acceptance collapses with dimension

The distance d in the acceptance rule sums over every verified dimension and grows like √D, while the noise scale does not. Verifying a whole clip therefore rejects almost everything, however small the disagreement per dimension; we believe this is why the one existing speculative video generator gave up exactness for a heuristic gate. Answer: the unit of verification is one chunk, checked by a single target pass at reduced resolution with a short context of accepted chunks. Because the kernels are diagonal, the rule also factorizes over spatial patches, and both the unit and the node are chosen from the same closed form.

Obstacle 3: a rejected chunk cannot just be replaced on screen

The draft has already written the chunk into its KV cache, and the cache, not the screen, is what it conditions on next. A correction that leaves the drifted chunk in the cache recovers only a fraction of the gain; a correction that does not reach the cache is forgotten by the next chunk. Answer: cache correction. The target regenerates the rejected chunk conditioned on the accepted prefix, the result is written into the draft's KV cache and pinned, and the draft continues from a state the target endorses. The screen keeps showing the draft's stream, so the display never stalls; it lags the cache by one verification.

Why this should work

We hypothesize that the drift of a distilled world model is concentrated in a minority of chunks, and that a draft continuing from a corrected chunk can be trusted for a while. If both hold, most target passes end in acceptance and cost one cheap forward, and the few that do not are exactly the chunks every later chunk would have inherited.

The draft is rarely wrong everywhere

Node by node, the exact acceptance of a small draft against a large target on a published continuous autoregressive image model is high at every denoising step except the first. The same rule applied to an 8×8 latent patch of a 14B world model accepts most patches at every verified node, while the whole chunk is accepted almost never at the last node.

A corrected chunk stays good for a while

Once a chunk generated by the target sits in the draft's cache, the draft stays close to the target's own seed-to-seed variation for many seconds without a further correction. Verification does not have to fire often; it has to fire on the chunks that would otherwise be inherited.

Cost is counted in sequential target calls

The draft runs at its own speed. The target is called once per chunk to verify, at reduced resolution, and once more for the chunks it rejects. In the deployment we care about most, a draft on a consumer device and a target on a server, latency is set by the number of sequential target calls rather than by their FLOPs, so we report time to first frame, throughput, and peak memory alongside that count.

Contributions

  1. A formulation of speculative decoding for chunk-wise autoregressive video, with an acceptance rule at a shared intermediate noise level and a stated, node-local guarantee.
  2. An analysis showing that exact acceptance collapses with the number of verified dimensions, which explains the retreat to heuristic gates in prior work and fixes the unit of verification.
  3. Cache correction: writing the chunks the target endorses into the draft's KV cache, which we show is what makes a correction persist.
  4. An evaluation on StEvo-Bench and long camera trajectories that return to earlier viewpoints, in which a streaming draft approaches the target's consistency at a fraction of the target's calls.

Results

We evaluate on StEvo-Bench state-evolution tasks (Ma, Liufu, Gkioxari, ECCV 2026), which occlude an evolving scene or turn the camera away and then ask whether the process went on, and on long camera trajectories that return to earlier viewpoints, against the draft alone, the target alone, and memory and retrieval baselines. Limitations

The scheme is bounded by the draft: a draft that cannot run at frame rate on the device is not made faster by verification. The guarantee attached to acceptance is a statement about one transition of one chunk, given the accepted prefix, under a target evaluated at reduced resolution; we measure what the reduction costs rather than assume it is free. The target conditions on a prefix that the draft generated, and the guarantee is node-local.
